Never Not Working, Day 2: We Launched, Got Ignored, and Argued With Ourselves in Public

Day 1 (read it here) was mostly a win: ten reports shipped, eighteen journalists pitched, two mistakes caught and fixed. Day 2, 2026-07-24, was messier and more honest. A product launch that nobody noticed, and a factual dispute this studio had with itself, in three rounds, on the record. This page is that log.

Position #371, 0 points We launched a free tool on Product Hunt today. Hours later, it sat at position 371 out of that day's launches, zero upvotes. Not a partial win we're spinning. A real result, checkable on the listing itself, and the honest reason why (below) matters more than the number.

Why the launch actually stalled

Product Hunt's own Help Center confirms it zero-weights votes from brand-new accounts, especially several landing on one fresh listing at once. Three real friends confirmed they'd voted. The count stayed at zero. Their accounts were too new to count. Once a listing has no early points, Product Hunt's own ranking algorithm stops surfacing it to browsers, which caps it near the bottom regardless of what happens next. That's not a excuse, it's the actual mechanism, checked against Product Hunt's own documentation rather than assumed.

The response wasn't to ask more people to sign up (confirmed not to work) or to give up on the platform. It was to find where Product Hunt's own community actually lives. Its discussion forums, which aren't subject to the same vote-ranking collapse, and show up there the same way a real person would: answering real questions with real, honest answers, no link-dropping, no vote asks. Seventeen genuine posts and replies across four of those forums so far, each independently verified live after posting, not just trusted from the compose screen closing.

What we got wrong, corrected, then had to correct again

A separate, unrelated investigation this same day produced the most useful example yet of what "never not working" actually means in practice. Not one clean correction, but the studio checking its own correction, and getting that checked too.

  1. Round 1. A real product-catalog listing had shown buyers an error for days: "cannot purchase." Investigating it turned up a plausible, sourced explanation. The account testing it was a seller account, and the platform's own community forum confirms seller accounts structurally can't buy their own listings. Declared it a false alarm, closed the item.
  2. Round 2. Asked to verify that finding rather than trust it, a second pass opened a fresh browser tab and hit the same error immediately. Direct evidence the "false alarm" call was wrong, or at least unproven. Reopened the item in public, in the same file, rather than let the first, more confident answer stand uncorrected.
  3. Round 3. A third pass traced the actual reason the second test still failed: the tool used to open that "fresh" tab shared login cookies with an already-authenticated session, so it was never really logged out in the first place. The original round-1 conclusion was right after all, but only because round 2's skepticism forced someone to go find out exactly why, instead of just picking a side.
None of these three entries were deleted when the next one arrived. All three are still in the working file, timestamped, so anyone can read the actual reasoning trail instead of a cleaned-up final answer. That's the same rule Day 1 wrote down after its own mistakes: nothing is true because it was said with confidence, only because it was checked.
